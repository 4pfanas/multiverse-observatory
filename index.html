02 · Superposition

Not here or there. Everywhere, until you look.

Picture someone crossing a street. Whether you watch or not, they have a position and a speed. An electron does not work that way. Until it is measured, the maths says its position is spread across every place it could be. That spread is a superposition.

A qubit is the smallest version: a thing that is 0 and 1 at once until it is measured.

Before observation, it has no single position.

03 · Measurement

Looking forces a single answer.

Put three qubits in superposition and they represent eight values at once: 000 through 111. Measure them and you get exactly one. Measure again from scratch and you may get a different one. Nothing in the maths says which.

05 · The branching tree

Find the one winning branch.

Four decisions means 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16 possible futures, and one of them is the winning one. At each step you pick a side and half the remaining futures drop away. You cannot see which leaf wins until you reach the end.

The sorcerer's 14,000,504 futures would need a tree about 24 decisions deep, because 224 is the first power of two that covers them.

07 · Circuit builder

Build the 14-qubit circuit.

A Hadamard gate (H) puts one qubit into an even superposition of 0 and 1. The article's circuit puts an H on all 14 wires, then measures every wire. Toggle the gates yourself.

The winning number is 1,473. Written in 14 bits, a wire with a 1 needs an H, or it can never come out as 1.

09 · The real physics

What the story simplifies.

The multiverse framing is a good hook. These notes keep it honest.

A register does not store 16,384 usable numbers

14 qubits in superposition carry 16,384 amplitudes, but a measurement returns only one 14-bit result. You cannot read the whole list back out. Useful quantum algorithms arrange the amplitudes so the answer you want becomes likely when you measure.

Many worlds does not count universes to decide who wins

"The heroes win in 1 of 14,000,504 worlds, so the villain wins nearly everywhere" treats every branch as equally weighted. In the many-worlds interpretation, branches carry amplitudes, and the Born rule links them to probabilities. Counting branches is a story device, not how the theory assigns likelihood.

Why this circuit is just a random number generator

H on every wire followed by measurement produces uniform random 14-bit numbers. That is useful for showing superposition and collapse, but it is not a quantum speed-up. Finding one marked number among 16,384 this way is the same as guessing.

Copenhagen and many worlds make the same predictions

Interpretations differ in what they say is real, not in what an experiment on this circuit will output. Your measurement histogram looks the same in both.
